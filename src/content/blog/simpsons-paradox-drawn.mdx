A chart of 40 cities shows one clear trend. Cities that spend more on
marketing grow less. The line points down. Then you divide the cities into
two groups, big and small. In each group, the line points up. Nothing in the
data changed. Only the grouping changed.

This is Simpson's paradox. A trend in the full data can reverse when you
divide the data into groups. The paradox is not an error in the math. Both
lines are correct. They answer different questions, and the rest of this
post is about which question is which.

## Why the line turns

The two groups differ on both axes. Big cities spend more per person, and
they grow less, because their markets are closer to saturation. Small cities
spend less and grow more. When all cities are on one chart, the pooled line
mostly compares big cities with small cities. It does not compare spend with
growth.

In each group, the cities have a similar size. The size effect is gone. What
is left is the effect of spend, and that effect is positive.

<SimpsonScatter
  mode="gap"
  caption="Move the slider to make the two groups more similar. When the groups overlap, the line for all cities agrees with the group lines."
/>

The reversal needs a third variable. That variable must move with the x-axis
and with the y-axis at the same time. City size does both. If the groups have
the same spend and the same growth, the paradox cannot occur. Move the slider
to zero and the three lines agree.

## A real case

In 1973, the University of California, Berkeley admitted 44 percent of male
applicants and 35 percent of female applicants. In most departments, women
were admitted at the same rate as men or at a higher rate. Women applied more
often to the departments with low admission rates. The department was the
hidden variable. Bickel, Hammel, and O'Connell published the analysis in
Science in 1975.

The same pattern appears in medical trials, where the sicker patients get
the stronger treatment, and in sales data, where the discounts go to the
customers who were about to leave.

## Which line answers your question

The group line answers a causal question. If one city increases its spend,
what happens to its growth? The city does not change size, so the comparison
must hold size fixed. The group line is the answer, and it points up.

The pooled line answers a prediction question. A new city sends you its spend
and nothing else. What growth do you expect? High spend is a sign of a big
city, and big cities grow less. The pooled line is the answer, and it points
down.

Most business questions are causal questions. "Does marketing work?" means
"What happens if we spend more?". It does not mean "What do high spenders
look like?". Answer it with the group line.

## How to find it before it finds you

A pooled trend is safe only when the groups are similar on the x-axis. Three
checks:

1. **Plot by group before you pool.** If the group lines and the pooled line
   disagree in direction, stop and explain why.
2. **Compare the x-axis by group.** If big cities spend 55 to 95 and small
   cities spend 10 to 50, the ranges do not overlap. The pooled line compares
   groups, not spend.
3. **Compare the group mix across arms.** If one arm of an A/B test has more
   big cities than the other, the aggregate result is not a fair comparison.

When the direction flips, report both lines and say which question each one
answers. Do not pick the one that supports the plan.

The data on this page is simulated. The pattern is real. Every dataset with a
hidden group has the same trap.
