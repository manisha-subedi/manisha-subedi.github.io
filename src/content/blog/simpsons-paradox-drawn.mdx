Let's say you have a chart of 40 cities. On the x-axis, how much each city
spends on marketing per person. On the y-axis, how much its sales grew this
year. You draw a trend line, and it points down. More marketing, less growth.
That is a strange result.

Then a colleague asks: are these cities all the same size? They are not.
Twenty are small and twenty are large. So you color the dots by size and
draw one line for each group. Now both lines point up.

Same 40 dots. Nothing changed except the color. And the story turned around,
from "marketing hurts" to "marketing helps". This is called Simpson's
paradox. After you see it one time, you will look for it in every chart that
has hidden groups inside.

## Why the line turns around

Let me explain it with numbers you can do in your head.

Small cities spend about 30 euros per person and grow about 3.5 percent.
Large cities spend about 75 euros per person and grow less than 2 percent.
Large cities spend more and grow less, because their market is already full.

Now put all 40 cities on one chart and ask for one line. What does that line
see? On the left, small cities with high growth. On the right, large cities
with low growth. So the line goes down. But it is not comparing "spend more"
with "spend less". It is comparing small cities with large cities. City size
does all the work.

Inside each group, the cities are about the same size. So size does not
matter anymore. What is left is spend against growth. And inside each group,
the cities that spend more do grow more.

So both lines are right. The grey line answers "do the high spenders grow
faster?" No, because the high spenders are the large cities. The colored
lines answer "if a city spends more, does it grow more?" Yes. Different
question, different line.

## Play with it

The paradox needs a third thing that pushes on both axes at the same time.
Here, city size pushes spend up and growth down. Take that away, and the
paradox goes away too. Move the slider to make the two groups more similar,
and watch the grey line turn.

## The same thing in Python

```python
import numpy as np

small_spend  = np.random.uniform(10, 50, 20)
small_growth = 2 + 0.05 * small_spend + np.random.normal(0, 0.4, 20)

big_spend  = np.random.uniform(55, 95, 20)
big_growth = -2 + 0.05 * big_spend + np.random.normal(0, 0.4, 20)  # same slope, lower start

slope = lambda x, y: np.polyfit(x, y, 1)[0]
slope(small_spend, small_growth)   # about +0.05
slope(big_spend, big_growth)       # about +0.05
slope(np.concatenate([small_spend, big_spend]),
      np.concatenate([small_growth, big_growth]))   # negative
```

Two groups with the same positive slope, and the line through all of them
points the other way. That is the whole trick.

## Where this can catch you

Any time your data has groups inside it. Customer segments, countries, phone
or desktop, old customers and new customers, weekdays and weekends. The habit
that saves you is cheap. Before you trust a trend line, color the dots by the
most obvious group. If the group lines agree with the overall line, fine. If
they turn around, you just found the real story. Put that group in the chart,
not in a footnote.

One more thing. A trend line shows that two things move together. It does
not show that one causes the other.
