A chart of 40 cities shows one clear trend. Cities that spend more on
marketing grow less. The line points down. Then you divide the cities into
two groups, big and small. In each group, the line points up. Nothing in the
data changed. Only the grouping changed.

  mode="toggle"
  caption="Simulated data. Each dot is one city. Use the checkbox to color the dots by city size."
/>

This is Simpson's paradox. A trend in the full data can reverse when you
divide the data into groups. The paradox is not an error in the math. Both
lines are correct. They answer different questions.

## Why the line turns

The two groups differ on both axes. Big cities spend more per person, and they
grow less. Small cities spend less, and they grow more. When all cities are on
one chart, the line mostly compares big cities with small cities. It does not
compare spend with growth.

In each group, the cities have a similar size. The size effect is gone. What
is left is the effect of spend, and that effect is positive.

<SimpsonScatter
  mode="gap"
  caption="Move the slider to make the two groups more similar. When the groups overlap, the line for all cities agrees with the group lines."
/>

The reversal needs a third variable. That variable must move with the x-axis
and with the y-axis at the same time. City size does both. If the groups have
the same spend and the same growth, the paradox cannot occur.

## What to do with a trend

Before you trust a trend, ask what else is different between the dots. Then
divide the data by that variable. If the direction stays the same, the trend
is stronger. If the direction changes, the variable is part of the answer.
Put it in the report. Do not delete it.

Common hidden variables are customer segment, device type, country, season,
and store size.

The data on this page is simulated. The pattern is real. It appears in
medical trials, admissions data, and sales reports.
