This chart compares marketing spend per person with sales growth in
40 simulated cities. The overall trend slopes down: cities with higher
spending tend to have lower growth.

The data contains 20 small cities and 20 large ones. Group them by size
and both trend lines slope up.

The observations have not changed. Within each group, higher spending is
associated with higher growth, even though the combined trend points the
other way. This reversal is an example of Simpson's paradox.

## Why the trend reverses

In this example, small cities spend about 30 euros per person and grow
about 3.5 percent. Large cities spend about 75 euros per person and grow
less than 2 percent.

The overall line reflects this difference between groups. The small
cities sit towards the upper left of the chart, while the large cities
sit towards the lower right.

Within each group, spending and growth have a positive relationship.
The combined line and group lines describe different comparisons; neither
one alone explains what would happen if a city increased its spending.

## Adjusting the groups

Move the slider to reduce the difference between small and large cities.
As the groups become more similar, the overall trend moves closer to the
trends within each group.

## The same example in Python

```python
import numpy as np

small_spend  = np.random.uniform(10, 50, 20)
small_growth = 2 + 0.05 * small_spend + np.random.normal(0, 0.4, 20)

big_spend  = np.random.uniform(55, 95, 20)
big_growth = -2 + 0.05 * big_spend + np.random.normal(0, 0.4, 20)  # same slope, lower start

slope = lambda x, y: np.polyfit(x, y, 1)[0]
slope(small_spend, small_growth)   # about +0.05
slope(big_spend, big_growth)       # about +0.05
slope(np.concatenate([small_spend, big_spend]),
      np.concatenate([small_growth, big_growth]))   # negative
```

Both groups use the same positive slope, but different starting levels.
The combined data can therefore have a negative slope.

## Checking grouped data

When analysing a trend, look for groups that may affect the comparison:
customer segments, countries, device types, or new and returning customers.
Plotting those groups separately can reveal a pattern that the overall
line hides.

A reversal is a reason to investigate the groups and how the data was
collected. It does not, by itself, show which relationship is causal.
