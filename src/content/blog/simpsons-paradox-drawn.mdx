Picture this. You have a chart of 40 cities. On the x-axis, how much each
city spends on marketing per person. On the y-axis, how much its sales grew
this year. You draw the trend line and it points down. More marketing, less
growth. Your manager is going to love this.

Then a colleague asks: are these cities all the same size? They are not.
Twenty are big, twenty are small. So you colour the dots by size, draw one
line per group, and both lines point up.

Same 40 dots. Nothing changed except the colour. And the story flipped from
"marketing hurts" to "marketing helps". This is Simpson's paradox, and once
you have seen it once, you will look for it in every chart that has a hidden
group inside.

## Why the line flips

Let me explain it the way I would to a friend, with numbers you can do in
your head.

Small cities spend about 30 euros per person and grow about 3.5 percent. Big
cities spend about 75 euros per person and grow under 2 percent. Big cities
spend more and grow less, simply because their market is already full.

Now put all 40 on one chart and ask for one line. What does that line see? On
the left, small cities with high growth. On the right, big cities with low
growth. So the line goes down. But it is not comparing "spend more" with
"spend less". It is comparing small cities with big cities. Size is doing all
the work, and spend is just along for the ride.

Inside each group, the cities are about the same size, so size is out of the
picture. What is left is spend against growth. And within each group, the
cities that spend more do grow more.

So both lines are right. The grey line answers "do the high spenders grow
faster?" No, because the high spenders are big cities. The coloured lines
answer "if a city spends more, does it grow more?" Yes. Different question,
different line.

## Play with it

The paradox needs a third thing that pushes on both axes at once. Here, city
size pushes spend up and growth down. Take that away and the paradox goes
with it. Move the slider to make the two groups more alike and watch the grey
line turn around.

## The same thing in code

```python
import numpy as np

small_spend  = np.random.uniform(10, 50, 20)
small_growth = 2 + 0.05 * small_spend + np.random.normal(0, 0.4, 20)

big_spend  = np.random.uniform(55, 95, 20)
big_growth = -2 + 0.05 * big_spend + np.random.normal(0, 0.4, 20)  # same slope, lower start

slope = lambda x, y: np.polyfit(x, y, 1)[0]
slope(small_spend, small_growth)   # about +0.05
slope(big_spend, big_growth)       # about +0.05
slope(np.concatenate([small_spend, big_spend]),
      np.concatenate([small_growth, big_growth]))   # negative
```

Two groups with the same positive slope, and the line through all of them
points the other way. That is the whole trick.

## Where this bites you

Any time your data has groups inside it. Customer segments, countries,
device types, old customers versus new ones, weekdays versus weekends. The
habit that saves you is cheap: before you trust a trend line, colour the dots
by the most obvious group. If the group lines agree with the overall line,
fine. If they flip, you just found the real story, and that group belongs in
the chart, not in a footnote.

And the next time a chart tells you marketing hurts sales, ask who the dots
are before you ask what the line says.
