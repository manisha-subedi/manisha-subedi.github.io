Everybody knows the average can lie. But when you try to explain why, it
turns into a lecture, and people stop listening. So here is how I explain
it. Six small questions, with numbers you can do in your head.

Imagine you have a small online shop. Last month you had 30 customers. 29 of
them bought for 10 euros. One of them, a wholesale buyer, bought for 1,000
euros.

**Q1. What is the total revenue?**

29 times 10 is 290. Plus 1,000. So 1,290 euros. Easy.

**Q2. Divide the total by the number of customers. What does the "average
customer" spend?**

1,290 divided by 30 is 43 euros.

**Q3. How many of the 30 customers spent around 43 euros?**

Zero. 29 of them spent less than half of that. One spent 23 times more. The
average customer spends 43 euros, and does not exist.

**Q4. Next month the wholesale buyer does not come back. The other 29 do
exactly the same as before. What is the average now?**

290 divided by 29 is 10 euros. The average dropped by 77 percent. Not one
customer changed anything. Only one customer left.

<MeanBalanceFigure />

**Q5. Put the 30 customers in a line, from smallest spend to biggest. Look at
the one in the middle. What did they spend?**

10 euros. With 30 people, the middle is between number 15 and number 16, and
both spent 10. Now take the wholesale buyer out of the line and look at the
middle again. Still 10. This middle number is called the median. It does not
care how big the biggest customer is.

<MedianLineFigure />

**Q6. Your manager wants one number for the slide. Which one do you give?**

It depends on the question behind the slide. "How much money do we make per
customer?" is a finance question. 43 is the honest answer, because it is the
total spread over everyone. "What does a customer usually spend?" is a
different question. The honest answer is 10. If you can only say one
sentence, say this: "A typical customer spends 10 euros, one customer spent
1,000, and revenue was 1,290." Three numbers, no surprise later.

## The same thing in code

```python
spend = [10] * 29 + [1000]

mean = sum(spend) / len(spend)            # 43.0
median = sorted(spend)[len(spend) // 2]   # 10

without_whale = spend[:-1]
sum(without_whale) / len(without_whale)   # 10.0
sorted(without_whale)[len(without_whale) // 2]   # 10
```

## Where this can catch you

This is not only about shops. It is about anything that has no top limit.
Salaries, where one director pulls the average up. Time on a website, where
one person left a tab open all night. Delivery time, where one parcel got
lost for three weeks. File sizes, order values, page load times. When the
biggest value can be a hundred times the normal one, the average follows the
biggest value. The median stays with the crowd.

The habit is simple. Every time you compute an average, compute the median
next to it. If the two are close, fine, the average tells the truth. If they
are far apart, look at the top five values before you put the average on a
slide. One of them is probably the whole story.

So the average customer spends 43 euros and does not exist. The median
customer spends 10 euros and is real. Show your manager the real one.
