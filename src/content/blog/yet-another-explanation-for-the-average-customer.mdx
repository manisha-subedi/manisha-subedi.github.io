This example has 30 customers. Of these, 29 spend 10 euros and one wholesale
buyer spends 1,000 euros. Six questions show what the mean and median tell us
about these values.

**Q1. What is the total revenue?**

29 times 10 is 290. Add 1,000 and the total is 1,290 euros.

**Q2. What is the mean spend per customer?**

1,290 divided by 30 is 43 euros.

**Q3. How many of the 30 customers spent close to 43 euros?**

None. The 29 customers who spent 10 euros each spent less than half of that
amount. The wholesale buyer spent more than 23 times the mean of 43 euros.
The mean is correct, but it is not an observed customer value. It is the
total divided equally across all 30 customers.

**Q4. In the next month, the wholesale buyer does not return. The other 29
customers spend the same amount as before. What is the mean now?**

The total is 290 euros. Dividing 290 by 29 gives a mean of 10 euros. The mean
fell by 77 percent, although none of the remaining customers changed their
spending. Only one customer left.

<MeanBalanceFigure />

**Q5. Sort the 30 customers from the smallest spend to the largest. What is
the middle value?**

With 30 customers, the middle lies between positions 15 and 16. Both values
are 10 euros, so the median is 10 euros. Remove the wholesale buyer and there
are 29 customers. The middle value is still 10 euros.

The median depends on the position of the middle value. It does not depend on
how far the largest value is from the rest.

<MedianLineFigure />

**Q6. Which number should a report use?**

It depends on the question. The mean of 43 euros answers "How much revenue did
we receive per customer?" because it divides total revenue by all customers.
The median of 10 euros answers "What did a typical customer spend?"

A short report could say: "A typical customer spent 10 euros. One customer
spent 1,000 euros. Total revenue was 1,290 euros." These three numbers show
both the usual purchase and the large order.

## Calculate both measures in Python

```python
spend = [10] * 29 + [1000]

mean = sum(spend) / len(spend)            # 43.0
median = sorted(spend)[len(spend) // 2]   # 10

without_whale = spend[:-1]
sum(without_whale) / len(without_whale)   # 10.0
sorted(without_whale)[len(without_whale) // 2]   # 10
```

## Other data with very large values

This issue can appear in any measure without a fixed upper limit. In salary
data, one director can raise the mean. In session data, one person who leaves
a tab open overnight can increase the mean session length. In delivery data,
one parcel delayed for three weeks can increase the mean delivery time. File
sizes, order values, and page load times can have the same pattern.

When the largest value is 100 times the typical value, the mean moves towards
that large value. The median remains near the middle of the observations.

Calculate the mean and median together. If they are far apart, inspect the
distribution and the top five values before reporting the mean by itself.

In this example, no customer spent 43 euros. Of the 30 customers, 29 spent
10 euros and one spent 1,000 euros. The mean of 43 euros and the median of
10 euros are both correct, but they answer different questions.
