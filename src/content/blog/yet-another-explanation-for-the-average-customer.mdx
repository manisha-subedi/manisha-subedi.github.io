Everybody knows that the average can mislead. But when you try to explain
it to someone, it turns into a lecture about distributions and tails and they
stop listening. So here is the way I do it: as a series of small questions,
with numbers you can do in your head.

Imagine you run a small online shop. Last month you had 30 customers. 29 of
them bought something for 10 euros. One of them, a wholesale buyer, bought
for 1,000 euros.

**Q1. What is the total revenue?**

29 times 10 is 290, plus 1,000. So 1,290 euros. Easy.

**Q2. Divide the total by the number of customers. What does the "average
customer" spend?**

1,290 divided by 30 is 43 euros.

**Q3. How many of the 30 customers spent around 43 euros?**

Zero. 29 of them spent less than half of that. One spent 23 times more. The
average customer spends 43 euros and does not exist.

**Q4. Next month the wholesale buyer does not come back. The other 29 do
exactly what they did before. What is the average now?**

290 divided by 29 is 10 euros. The average dropped by 77 percent, and not a
single customer changed their behaviour. Only one left.

<MeanBalanceFigure />

**Q5. Line up the 30 customers from smallest spend to biggest, and look at
the one in the middle. What did they spend?**

10 euros. With 30 people the middle is between number 15 and number 16, and
both spent 10. Now take the wholesale buyer out of the line and look at the
middle again. Still 10. This middle number is the median, and it does not
care how big the biggest customer is.

<MedianLineFigure />

**Q6. Your manager wants one number for the slide. Which one do you give?**

It depends on the question behind the slide. "How much money do we make per
customer?" is a finance question, and 43 is the honest answer, because it is
the total spread out evenly. "What does a customer usually spend?" is a
different question, and the honest answer is 10. If you have to pick one
sentence, pick this one: "A typical customer spends 10 euros, one customer
spent 1,000, and revenue was 1,290." Three numbers, no surprise later.

## The same thing in code

```python
spend = [10] * 29 + [1000]

mean = sum(spend) / len(spend)            # 43.0
median = sorted(spend)[len(spend) // 2]   # 10

without_whale = spend[:-1]
sum(without_whale) / len(without_whale)   # 10.0
sorted(without_whale)[len(without_whale) // 2]   # 10
```

## Where this bites you

This is not a story about shops. It is a story about anything that has no
ceiling. Salaries, where one director pulls the average up. Session length,
where one person left a tab open overnight. Delivery time, where one parcel
got lost for three weeks. File sizes, order values, page load times. When the
biggest value can be a hundred times the typical one, the average follows the
biggest value, and the median stays with the crowd.

The habit that saves you is simple. Whenever you compute an average, compute
the median next to it. If the two are close, fine, the average is telling the
truth. If they are far apart, look at the top five values before you put the
average on a slide. One of them is probably the whole story.

So, the average customer spends 43 euros and does not exist. The median
customer spends 10 euros and is real. Introduce your manager to the real one.
