Your team ran an A/B test. Button A got 50 percent clicks, button B got 55
percent, with 200 users on each. Somebody says "B wins, ship it". Do you
believe it?

I don't, and I want to show you why with bouncing balls instead of
statistics.

<BallBox />

Here is a box with 20 balls bouncing around. Nothing pushes them left or
right, so the true share of balls in the shaded half is 50 percent. Watch
the counter. It says 45, then 60, then 35. Now press "500 balls". The counter
calms down and sits between 47 and 53.

Nothing changed about the physics. The only thing that changed is how many
balls you count. That is the whole secret of sample size, and you just saw
it.

## Idea 1: noise has a size, and you can predict it

The wobble of a share shrinks as the count grows, but slowly. The rule is:
the wobble is about the square root of p(1−p)/n, where p is the true share
and n is the count. Sounds scary, so let's just do it. For p = 0.5, p(1−p)
is 0.25.

- 20 balls: sqrt(0.25 / 20) = 0.11. The counter wobbles by about 11 points.
- 200 balls: sqrt(0.25 / 200) = 0.035. About 3.5 points.
- 2,000 balls: about 1.1 points.

Ten times more balls, only three times less wobble. That is the annoying
part of the square root, and it is why sample sizes always feel too big.

## Idea 2: a test asks "is the difference bigger than the wobble?"

Back to the A/B test. 200 users on each button, so each button's click share
wobbles by about 3.5 points. The difference between two wobbly numbers
wobbles more, by a factor of about 1.4, so about 5 points.

Look at that again. B is ahead by 5 points, and 5 points is exactly the size
of the wobble when nothing is going on. Two identical buttons will produce a
"5 point win" for one of them about one time in six. Don't take my word for
it, simulate it:

```python
import random

def share(n, p=0.5):
    return sum(random.random() < p for _ in range(n)) / n

# 1,000 A/B tests where the two buttons are exactly the same
wins = sum(share(200) - share(200) >= 0.05 for _ in range(1000))
print(wins / 1000)    # about 0.16
```

One test in six says "B wins by 5" when there is nothing to win. That is
your 55 versus 50.

## Idea 3: now invent the formula

You want the wobble of the difference to be clearly smaller than the effect
you care about. Say, a third of it, so a real effect stands out and a fake
one does not. The wobble of the difference is sqrt(2 · p(1−p) / n). Set it
equal to d/3, solve for n, and you get:

n = 18 · p(1−p) / d²

For p = 0.5 and a 5 point effect, that is 18 × 0.25 / 0.0025 = 1,800 users
per button. Not 200.

```python
def users_per_arm(p, d):
    return 18 * p * (1 - p) / d**2

users_per_arm(0.5, 0.05)   # 1800
users_per_arm(0.5, 0.02)   # 11250, a 2 point effect is expensive
```

The textbook formula has 16 where I have 18, because statisticians chose
their "clearly smaller" more carefully than I did. If you want the words, it
is a 5 percent false alarm rate and 80 percent power. The shape is the same.
You could have invented it in a box with some balls.

## Where this bites you

Every time a share is computed on a small count. Conversion rate by country,
where one country had 30 visitors. Defect rate per supplier, where one
supplier shipped 12 parts. The "best performing" ad, store, or day of the
week. The smallest group wobbles the most, so the extreme values in a ranking
are usually the smallest groups, not the best or the worst ones.

The habit: before you compare two shares, compute the wobble,
sqrt(p(1−p)/n), for the smaller group. If the difference is not at least two
or three wobbles, you have a coin flip with a chart on top.

And if anyone tells you B wins with 200 users, send them the box and tell
them to press "20 balls".
